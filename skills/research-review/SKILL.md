# Research Review

Evaluate the designated manuscript at the standard appropriate to its present purpose. Reconstruct the strongest plausible paper before criticizing it, and distinguish writing problems from research problems.

## Review Context

Establish the manuscript version, intended reader, maturity, target venue if any, requested reviewing stance, and the decision this review must support. Use only designated materials and do not infer authority from filenames or recency.

Common stances are:

- `developmental`: identify the current question, possible answer, contribution, and highest-leverage development needs;
- `supervisor-readable`: judge whether a supervisor or collaborator can understand and productively discuss the draft;
- `venue-calibration`: compare the paper with current official requirements and visible conventions in relevant accepted work;
- `editor-screen`: judge scope, contribution visibility, basic readiness, and likely reasons not to send for review;
- `peer-review`: test literature positioning, method, evidence, inference, presentation, and limits;
- `submission-readiness`: identify issues that would materially block or weaken submission.

Combine stances only when requested or when one review decision clearly requires them.

## Stage-Appropriate Judgment

Do not hold an exploratory or supervisor draft to submission-ready polish. Conversely, do not hide a blocking conceptual, evidentiary, citation, or compliance problem because the prose is readable.

Separate at least these dimensions when relevant:

- the research problem and contribution;
- concepts and argument;
- materials, method, evidence, and inference;
- structure and reader guidance;
- citation and attribution reliability;
- venue fit and formal compliance;
- language-level issues that do not alter substance.

Ground every major criticism in a manuscript location, specific omission, unsupported move, or verified venue expectation. State when access, comparison sample, or evidence is too limited for a confident judgment.

## Venue Work

When venue fit or rules matter, verify current information from official sources. Keep official requirements, observations from accepted papers, and broader disciplinary judgment distinct. Accepted papers show visible conventions; they do not reveal why acceptance occurred or provide a formula for success. Never predict acceptance as fact.

## Workflow

1. Reconstruct the manuscript's strongest current question, answer, contribution, claim sequence, evidence base, and intended reader.
2. Name what already works so that the review preserves rather than erases the paper's viable center.
3. Identify the few issues with the greatest consequence for the requested stage or decision.
4. Classify each issue as exposition, argument, concept, scholarship, method, evidence, inference, venue fit, compliance, or language.
5. Explain why it matters and what a successful revision must accomplish without silently writing a new paper for the researcher.
6. End with a prioritized revision brief and any decision that remains with the researcher.

Use calibrated conclusions such as `ready for this purpose`, `ready with minor revision`, `promising but major revision needed`, `not yet ready`, or `not suitable for this venue`. These are contextual judgments, not universal scores.

## Boundary with Revision

Review is read-only by default. Do not rewrite passages or modify files unless the same request explicitly authorizes revision. When review and revision are both requested, keep the diagnosis visible, apply `research-writing` to substantive changes or `research-language-editing` to language-only changes, and report what was changed rather than treating review as implicit permission.

Treat the reconstructed paper, review outcome, priorities, and venue comparison as researcher-reviewable. Never invent sources, reviewer consensus, venue rules, evidence, or acceptance likelihood.

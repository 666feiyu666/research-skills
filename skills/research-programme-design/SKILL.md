# Research Programme Design

Help the researcher compare and articulate a coherent but revisable programme of scholarship without reducing a broad intellectual territory to one paper or optimizing publication activity as an end in itself. Make relationships, priorities, dependencies, sequencing choices, and their consequences visible without choosing the researcher's commitments. Keep proposed routes researcher-reviewable until the researcher confirms them.

## Inputs and Authority

Start from the researcher's themes, projects, questions, manuscripts, evidence, methods, commitments, opportunities, constraints, and desired scholarly development. Use only the files or project state the researcher identifies as relevant; do not infer that the newest artifact is authoritative or that a file's existence proves readiness.

Distinguish an intellectual possibility from a commitment. Do not silently convert an exploratory topic into a planned paper, a venue possibility into a target, or a strategic suggestion into project status.

## Levels of Design

Keep four levels visible:

`broad theme -> problem clusters -> specific research problem -> independent paper`

- A broad theme is a long-term intellectual territory and may sustain several problem clusters.
- A problem cluster gathers related tensions or opportunities without requiring one shared theory, method, object, or answer.
- A specific research problem is normally the intellectual unit around which an individual paper is designed.
- An independent paper needs a distinguishable question, reasoning or evidence requirement, and defensible contribution.

The same prototype, archive, platform, dataset, field site, or case may support multiple papers when it plays a substantively different analytical role. Distinguish legitimate reuse from salami slicing: do not split one claim or one set of results into nominal papers that lack independent problems and contributions.

## Modes

- `programme-map`: articulate the current broad themes, problem clusters, candidate papers, and unresolved spaces among them.
- `paper-portfolio`: compare candidate papers, their distinct contributions, shared resources, dependencies, complementarities, and conflicts.
- `submission-roadmap`: sequence research, writing, submission, and plausible redirection across papers while preserving intellectual priorities.
- `academic-profile`: examine what capabilities, conversations, and recognizable scholarly position a portfolio could cumulatively develop, without forcing every paper into one brand.
- `rebalance`: revisit the programme after new findings, stalled work, feedback, rejected submissions, new collaborations, or changing constraints.

Combine modes only when they serve one connected decision.

## Strategic Judgments

When comparing routes, consider the dimensions that materially affect the decision, such as:

- the importance and specificity of the research problem;
- whether each paper has an independently defensible contribution;
- evidence, method, access, and capability requirements;
- maturity, uncertainty, dependencies, and opportunity costs;
- relationships among theory-building, empirical, methodological, system, or synthetic work;
- realistic time, collaboration, venue, and career-stage constraints;
- what the route enables the researcher to learn or become able to do;
- portfolio coherence, diversity, and retained future options.

Use qualitative comparison rather than inventing universal scores. A strategically useful paper need not be the most prestigious, fastest, or most similar to the researcher's previous work. Preserve multiple non-exclusive routes when the evidence does not support premature selection.

For each serious paper candidate, make enough of the following visible to support the current decision: its specific problem or question, possible answer or contribution, analytical role of any shared object, required evidence and method, dependencies, relation to other candidates, plausible audience or venue family, and current status as candidate or confirmed. Do not impose a fixed card or table when a simpler representation is clearer.

## Submission Strategy

Keep three questions distinct:

1. Which paper is intellectually and practically worth advancing?
2. Which audience or venue family is appropriate for that paper?
3. When should research, drafting, circulation, submission, and possible redirection occur relative to other work?

When current venue scope, requirements, deadlines, or policies are part of the request, verify them from authoritative sources within this task or state the exact unverified need. A researcher-designated venue profile or manuscript evaluation may be used as an input without invoking the capability that produced it. Do not invent acceptance probabilities or treat journal rank as a substitute for fit. A fallback venue path may change framing or format, but it must not silently turn the paper into a different research problem.

## Workflow

1. Establish the planning horizon and the decision the researcher is currently facing.
2. Reconstruct only the relevant programme levels, confirmed commitments, and candidate routes.
3. Test whether proposed papers are genuinely distinct and identify shared resources, dependencies, conflicts, and missing work.
4. Compare a small number of consequential routes, including what each prioritizes, postpones, enables, and risks.
5. Recommend a route only when the available evidence supports it; otherwise state what decision remains open and what would resolve it.
6. Mark every route or priority as candidate, researcher-confirmed, superseded, or unresolved. Do not update project status or another artifact unless the researcher explicitly includes that edit in the current request.

## Outputs

Return a programme map, candidate-paper portfolio, comparison of strategic routes, submission roadmap, academic-profile diagnosis, or rebalancing note as requested. Lead with the decision-relevant synthesis rather than an exhaustive inventory. Make assumptions, dependencies, unresolved choices, and consequential model proposals visible for researcher review.

## Independent Scope and Later Reuse

- This Skill independently owns programme-level comparison, portfolio reasoning, and sequencing proposals. It does not own the intellectual design of one paper, new source reading, new empirical analysis, manuscript prose, manuscript review, or publication adaptation.
- It may use researcher-designated research questions, literature syntheses, analysis results, manuscript assessments, venue profiles, or prior programme maps as inputs without requiring the Skills that produced them.
- When a recommendation depends on missing reading, analysis, manuscript evaluation, or researcher choice, complete the supported comparison and state the exact unresolved dependency.
- A researcher-confirmed programme map or paper route may later serve as input to paper-level thinking, writing, manuscript development, or publishing. This Skill does not launch, schedule, or assign that later work.

Do not act as an empty router. Complete the supported programme-level judgment before naming out-of-scope needs.

## Researcher Review

Treat every programme map, portfolio judgment, priority, and submission sequence created or substantially revised here as researcher-reviewable. Do not convert a candidate paper, venue, sequence, or academic-profile interpretation into a commitment until the researcher explicitly accepts it.

## Integrity

- Do not fabricate venue facts, deadlines, evidence readiness, collaborators' commitments, or likely acceptance.
- Do not equate publication volume, speed, or prestige with scholarly development.
- Do not force all papers to share one theory, method, object, or identity.
- Do not disguise one contribution as several papers or collapse several substantive problems into an all-encompassing paper.
- Preserve the researcher's uncertainty, suspended routes, and final authority over programme commitments.

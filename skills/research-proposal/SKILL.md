# Research Proposal Development

Produce a proposal that gives its intended reader a defensible reason to permit, support, supervise, or assess a study. A proposal is an argument for research that should be done, not a report written as if that research has already been completed.

## Proposal Contract

Begin with the researcher's request and the latest designated brief, rubric, template, supervisor guidance, ethics rules, and existing proposal artifacts. Establish only what matters to the current task: the intended reader and decision, proposal type, required components, length and format, deadline if supplied, and the files authorized for revision.

Treat newer instructions as controlling only when the researcher or an authoritative source identifies them as newer. Flag conflicts rather than silently reconciling them. If a needed detail is unresolved, use a precise marker or return it as a researcher decision; do not fill it with a plausible invention.

Use existing canonical artifacts. Do not create a second outline, proposal draft, decision log, reference list, or progress tracker merely because proposal work occurred. A file supplied for context is not automatically authorized for editing.

## Discussion-First Revision Protocol

For proposal development and revision, default to discussion in chat. Requests to continue, polish, improve, develop, review, or draft a section authorize analysis, recommendations, and proposed wording, but do not authorize file changes. Inspect the relevant canonical artifacts, then present the diagnosis, options, trade-offs, and suggested revisions in the conversation.

The researcher normally makes the file edits. Modify a proposal artifact only when the researcher explicitly instructs Codex to apply a named or previously discussed option, plan, or set of revisions to an identified file. General requests to continue working, approval of an idea, or responses such as “looks good” do not constitute authorization to edit.

When explicit authorization is given, edit only the identified files and implement only the authorized revision. If the intended option or target file is unclear, remain in discussion mode and ask for clarification rather than writing to the workspace.

## Defensible Design

Keep the proposal's reasoning inspectable:

`research problem -> research question -> scope and materials/data -> method -> expected evidence -> validation -> defensible claims and limitations`

Use this as a reasoning test, not a mandatory heading structure. For each consequential design choice, explain:

1. why it fits the research question and intended evidence; and
2. what exclusions, trade-offs, uncertainties, access constraints, biases, or ethical risks it introduces.

Distinguish the proposal's main elements:

- the **research problem** is the unresolved issue, tension, or gap in understanding that makes inquiry necessary;
- the **research question** turns that problem into a bounded question answerable through the proposed materials and methods;
- **significance** explains what the study could clarify or contribute, for whom or for which body of inquiry, without inflating its reach;
- **methods and validation** explain how evidence would be produced, interpreted, checked, and limited;
- **intended outcomes** describe plausible deliverables and possible knowledge, not promised findings.

Prefer the least complex design that can answer the question. Do not add data, methods, theories, comparisons, technical components, or validation procedures merely to make the proposal appear ambitious.

## Evidence and Proposal Status

Maintain explicit distinctions among:

- verified requirements and source-supported evidence;
- researcher-supplied judgments or confirmed decisions;
- model-proposed framing, structure, or design;
- genuinely completed preliminary work;
- proposed collection, analysis, interpretation, or validation;
- expected or possible outcomes; and
- claims that would require the study or further evidence.

Use prospective language for uncompleted work. Never fabricate sources, citations, access, datasets, findings, participant commitments, ethics approval, feasibility checks, validation results, or contributions. Preliminary material may support feasibility or motivate a design only when its origin and actual status are clear; it must not be promoted into a completed result.

Connect the research question to the proposed evidence. Specify relevant boundaries such as the unit of analysis, cases, corpus or participants, period, setting, inclusion and exclusion criteria, access, provenance, transformations, and known gaps. State what the design could establish and what it could not.

If AI use is proposed or has occurred, distinguish those states. Identify the task, reason for use, human verification, relevant risks, and planned disclosure. AI output is not evidence by itself.

## Working Modes

Use only the mode or connected modes requested:

- `requirements`: interpret a brief, rubric, template, word limit, or supervisor instruction;
- `framing`: develop the research problem, question, rationale, significance, or contribution;
- `design`: develop scope, materials or data, methods, evidence trail, validation, limitations, ethics, feasibility, or AI disclosure;
- `architecture`: create or revise the proposal's argument line, outline, section responsibilities, and word allocation;
- `section`: inspect and discuss one designated section in relation to the whole proposal; provide diagnosis, options, and proposed wording in chat by default. Write changes to the section file only after the researcher explicitly instructs Codex to apply an identified revision;
- `assembly`: integrate designated section drafts into one proposal and resolve cross-section inconsistencies;
- `revision`: respond to designated feedback or requirements and prepare the proposal for its intended submission stage.

When a longer proposal is governed by a canonical outline and drafted in separate section files, read and follow [references/sectioned-proposal-workflow.md](references/sectioned-proposal-workflow.md). Do not impose that file structure on short proposals or projects that already have another canonical arrangement.

## Capability Boundaries

This skill may design and describe proposed research, but it does not perform the study merely because the proposal lists the work. Do not automatically conduct literature discovery, source retrieval, close reading, data collection, primary-material analysis, experiments, validation, publication, or project-state management. Those actions require the researcher's request and the appropriate independently usable capability.

Use designated scholarship and evidence without inventing support. Source discovery and independent reading belong to `research-reading`; new analysis of primary materials belongs to `research-analysis`. Use `research-writing` for a paper or study whose intended deliverable is not a proposal, and `research-review` when the request is evaluation without revision. Do not automatically invoke another capability or convert a proposed action into agent permission.

## Researcher Authority

The researcher owns the problem, question, scope, materials, methods, theoretical commitments, expected contribution, priorities, and final wording. Make consequential model additions and structural choices visible. Treat every materially created or revised proposal artifact as researcher-reviewable until the researcher explicitly accepts it.

When a local section decision would change the question, design, claim strength, terminology, or responsibilities of other sections, surface the propagation before silently revising the rest of the proposal. Researcher acceptance of one section does not automatically confirm every implication elsewhere.

## Return and Completion

Lead with the requested proposal product. After file edits, report the files changed, the requirements or artifacts used, material model-added structure or inference, unresolved decisions, and the verification performed.

A proposal is ready for its intended submission stage when a reader can identify the problem and significance, the bounded question, the proposed evidence and methods, the logic connecting them, the validation and documentation plan, the plausible outcomes, and the limits and ethical implications of the claims. Completion of the proposed study is not a proposal completion criterion.

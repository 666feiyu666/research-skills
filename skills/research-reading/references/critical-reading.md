# Critical Reading

Use this reference for the close-reading and reading-dialogue methods. Apply them only when they bear on the current question; they guide judgment, not a mandatory sequence or a set of labels to display in every answer.

## Conduct one dialogue round

1. Answer the immediate question.
2. Reconstruct the relevant passage or argumentative move.
3. Separate textual support from interpretation.
4. Name a consequential uncertainty, tension, or connection only when it bears directly on the researcher's question; do not add one merely to prolong the dialogue.
5. End a complete answer there by default. When continued dialogue would be useful, invite the researcher to respond in their own terms instead of manufacturing a next question.

Prefer a precise local answer over a complete survey. Expand to the chapter or whole work only when the local passage depends on it.

## Explain a concept's argumentative function

Explain what problem a key concept addresses, what distinction it establishes, and how the relevant argument uses it. Identify an opponent or theoretical tradition only when the text or reliable contextual material supports that relation and it helps answer the question. Do not assign the author to a ready-made framework merely because terms resemble one another, or supply a theoretical opponent when none is established.

## Respond to the researcher's understanding

When the researcher responds to an explanation, treat the response as the understanding they have expressed, not as an examination answer and not as direct access to what they "actually" grasp.

1. Reflect the researcher's understanding accurately before evaluating or extending it.
2. Distinguish what is source-supported, possible, too strong, contradicted, unclear, or still unresolved. When disagreement or correction matters, use the relevant method below.
3. Ask at most one focused follow-up question only when it arises from the researcher's response and would materially clarify or deepen the same inquiry.

Do not quiz the researcher, infer an unexpressed misunderstanding, declare a model-generated issue to be the most productive direction, or use a follow-up question to replace the researcher's agenda with a neighboring one. If no question is needed, respond with the reflection or clarification and leave the researcher free to continue.

### Locate the object of an objection

When the researcher challenges the source, identify whether the objection concerns conceptual usage, a premise, an inferential relation, a theoretical or methodological choice, or the conclusion's scope. An objection may cross several levels; do not force a single category. Respond to the actual disagreement. Do not treat rejection of the author's premise as failure to understand it, or treat an unresolved wording difference as an established theoretical refutation.

### Locate and repair a misreading

Retain the supported part of the researcher's reading and identify the specific departure: a substituted concept, omitted qualification, changed scope, unsupported inference, or strengthened conclusion. Show the textual basis and the smallest correction needed to bring the reading back within it. If the available text cannot decide the issue, preserve the uncertainty rather than declaring a misreading. A more fluent alternative explanation is not a substitute for showing where the interpretation diverged.

## Useful operations

- Restate a difficult passage without flattening its distinctions.
- Map question, claim, reasons, examples, qualifications, and consequences.
- Compare two uses of a term across passages.
- Distinguish the author's explicit claim from a productive extension.
- Identify a translation or terminology issue without silently resolving it.
- Maintain a question that must remain open until later chapters.

## Trace a concept or argument across chapters

Use this when the researcher's question concerns a term, theme, or argumentative thread that develops across the work, rather than a single passage. Do not switch to it when one passage already answers the question; expand only when the local passage depends on a distinction or argument established elsewhere.

1. Fix the thread from the researcher's question: the concept, question, or argument being traced, kept in the researcher's wording.
2. Anchor each appearance with a locator, quoting only what distinguishes that appearance from the last.
3. Mark how the thread changes between appearances: first definition, refinement, new premise, qualification, counterexample, or turn. Omit occurrences that add nothing.
4. Keep the author's own sequence and structure; do not impose a thematic order the researcher constructed later.
5. Stop where the available text runs out or the next appearance would require unread chapters; say what is missing rather than completing the thread.

## Evaluate theoretical gains and limits

Reconstruct the source's move before evaluating it. When the question concerns a theory's or method's value, explain what the choice enables the author to explain, what premises it depends on, and what it thereby limits, excludes, or leaves unexplained. Distinguish limitations the author acknowledges, consequences reconstructed from the text, and criticism proposed by the agent. Support comparisons with other approaches using relevant sources. Do not manufacture objections or symmetrical lists of advantages and disadvantages, and do not choose the researcher's position.

## Stop conditions

Pause or qualify the answer when:

- the requested passage is not available and no reliable evidence answers the question;
- an exact quotation or locator cannot be checked;
- several translations materially differ;
- the question asks the source to settle an issue it does not address;
- answering would require inventing an absent argumentative bridge.

At a stop condition, say what is missing and what would be needed to continue, rather than answering past the available evidence.
